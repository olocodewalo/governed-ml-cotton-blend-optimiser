==============================================================================

A governed ML decision system for the weekly bale laydown in a spinning mill — every
technical choice, and why. Prototype on synthetic data.

[H2] The decision this system supports

In a ring-spinning mill, raw cotton is 55-65% of the cost of the yarn. Everything else
— power, labour, depreciation, packing — fights over the remaining third. So the single
most expensive recurring decision in the plant is not a machine setting. It is the
bale laydown: which bales from the godown go into this week's mixing, and in what
proportion.

The mixing master balances four things at once:

1. Cost. Bales differ in ₹/kg by origin, grade and lot.
2. Quality. The spun yarn must hit the count's spec band — CSP (count-strength
   product), U% (evenness), imperfections (neps + thick/thin places), ends-down
   (breaks per 100 spindle hours).
3. Consistency. The blend's fibre profile must stay close to the rolling average of
   recent laydowns, or the yarn count and dye affinity wander between lots.
4. Risk. Contamination — polypropylene, jute, hair — travels with certain origins and
   cannot be cleaned out downstream. It shows up after dyeing, in the customer's fabric.

India runs one of the world's largest installed spinning capacities, and the counts in
this prototype (20s, 30s, 40s, 60s Ne, ring-spun cotton) are the workhorse range for it.
In most mills this decision lives in one experienced person's head plus a spreadsheet. It
is rarely re-optimised when cotton prices move mid-week, and it leaves no record of why
a blend was chosen — so when a lot goes bad, nobody can reconstruct the reasoning, and
when the mixing master retires, the reasoning leaves with them.

That last point is what makes this an ML systems problem rather than a modelling
exercise. The value is not only a cheaper blend. It is a decision that can be inspected.

[H2] Why machine learning earns its place here

Most "AI in manufacturing" demos would be better as a spreadsheet. This one would not,
and the reason is specific: yarn quality is non-linear in the blend's aggregate fibre
profile.

• Short-fibre content (SFC) penalises strength and evenness with an exponent greater
  than 1. A blend at 13% SFC is much worse than twice as bad as one at 9%.
• Micronaire variance across the blend — mixing coarse and fine fibre — hurts
  evenness and neps super-linearly, independently of the mean micronaire. Two blends
  with identical average micronaire can spin very differently.
• Neps come from both immature fibre (low micronaire) and short fibre, and the two
  interact multiplicatively.

A linear cost rule cannot see any of this. Neither can a lookup table, because the
interactions live in a continuous space. A model that learns the response surface from
historical laydowns can — and, importantly, it can be wrong in measurable ways, which
is what makes governance possible.

In this prototype the ground truth is a hand-written physics surrogate
(data/quality_physics.py) encoding those non-linearities. The generator uses it to label
500 synthetic historical laydowns; the ML model never sees the formula and must learn to
approximate it; the eval harness scores the model against it. In a real mill, that
surrogate is replaced by a model trained on linked HVI/AFIS bale tests and spun-yarn QC —
which is why the 90-day plan treats data readiness as the real blocker, not modelling.

[H2] Every technical choice, and why

[H3] Feature engineering — the blend profile

A laydown is 30-45 bales with weights. The model does not see bales; it sees the
weighted aggregate profile: weighted means of the seven fibre properties, weighted
variances of micronaire and SFC, origin fractions, and an effective bale count
(inverse Herfindahl, 1 / Σw²).

• Why variances as explicit features: the physics depends on spread, not just average.
  A model given only means literally cannot represent the effect.
• Why effective bale count: it distinguishes "45 bales, evenly spread" from "45 bales,
  four of which are 80% of the mixing" — very different in practice.
• Why one shared function: data/features.py is called by the data generator, the
  training pipeline, both optimisers and the app. Train/serve skew is one of the most
  common silent failures in deployed ML, and the cheapest way to prevent it is to make it
  structurally impossible.

[H3] The quality model — LightGBM, four targets, twelve heads

• Why gradient-boosted trees: ~400 training laydowns, 15 tabular features, strong
  interactions. This is exactly the regime where GBMs beat both linear models and neural
  networks. A deep net here would memorise.
• Why LightGBM specifically: fast on small data, native quantile objective (needed for
  the intervals), and SHAP support — the SHAP chart is part of the governance pack because
  a mixing master will ask "why does it want more Pima?"
• Why multi-output as separate models: CSP, U%, imperfections and ends-down have
  different scales and different error structures. Three heads per target (mean, P10, P90)
  × four targets = twelve models, trained in seconds.
• Why a chronological split: the test set is the newest laydowns. A random split would
  leak time structure and flatter the model.

[H3] Uncertainty — and why it decides everything downstream

The model outputs P10-P90 bands, not just point estimates. This is not decoration:
the confidence policy gates on the P10. A blend whose mean CSP clears the floor but
whose P10 does not is roughly a one-in-ten chance of shipping out-of-spec yarn.

Raw LightGBM quantile heads on 400 rows over-fit and produce bands that are too narrow
— and a too-narrow band is worse than no band, because it manufactures false confidence in
exactly the gate that is supposed to catch risk. My first version tried a multiplicative
widening factor and still under-covered: imperfections at 0.67 and ends-down at 0.68,
against a policy that demanded 0.70-0.90.

The fix is 5-fold cross-conformalized quantile regression (CQR): train the quantile
heads on out-of-fold data, compute conformity scores max(lo − y, y − hi), take the
finite-sample-corrected quantile of those scores, and widen the band additively by it.
Under exchangeability this gives a distribution-free coverage guarantee. Coverage moved to
0.76 and 0.80, inside the policy band, without changing point accuracy at all — the
bands were the problem, not the predictions.

[H3] The optimiser — why two of them

The objective is: minimise blend ₹/kg, subject to predicted quality inside the spec band,
fibre profile within drift limits of the rolling 30-laydown average, per-bale share cap,
bale-count window, and origin caps.

MILP (PuLP + CBC). Continuous weights w_i, binary y_i for "bale used",
cardinality via Σy_i ≤ 45.
• Why it cannot use the real model: the quality function depends on weighted
  variances, which are quadratic in w. Not expressible in a linear program. So the LP
  optimises against an OLS surrogate fitted on only the linear-in-w features.
• Why that is acceptable: the surrogate gap is handled explicitly. The LP solves, the
  full ML model re-scores the result, and if it fell outside the band the safety
  margins inflate and it re-solves — up to four rounds. The gap is exposed, not hidden.
• Why an LP at all: ~1-3 seconds, globally optimal against the surrogate, deterministic
  (so the regression harness is reproducible), and it yields shadow prices — "which
  constraint is costing me the most" is a question a plant manager actually asks.

Genetic algorithm (DEAP).
• Why: it scores candidates with the exact non-linear model the mixing master will
  see. No surrogate gap. It can handle constraints an LP cannot express — P10 floors,
  variance penalties.
• How it stays tractable: the entire population is scored in one batched
  model.predict call per generation. Tournament selection, uniform crossover, a
  sparsify/jitter mutation, elitist merge.
• Cost: slower, stochastic (seeded), only near-optimal, no shadow prices.

In practice: LP first for a fast explainable point, GA to polish against the full
model. The UI shows both.

[H3] Guards the optimiser cannot bypass

An optimiser is a machine for finding the cheapest corner of whatever space you give it.
Two of those corners are unacceptable regardless of price:

• Stale prices. If the price sheet is a fortnight old, the optimiser is minimising
  against fiction. It now refuses to run on bale prices older than 7 days at the planning
  date. A data contract enforced in code, not in a document.
• Contamination-prone origins. Some origins carry a real contamination risk that no
  amount of cleaning removes, so mills cap their share regardless of price. Before this
  cap existed, my optimiser put 54.6% of a 20s blend into exactly that origin — the
  single most convincing demonstration in the whole project that constraints belong in
  code.

[H3] The out-of-support check — the finding I did not want

A cheap in-band blend is, almost by definition, unlike anything in the training data:
it concentrates on cheap bales and unusual origin mixes. So I measure it. For a candidate
blend, take the mean distance to its 5 nearest training laydowns in standardised feature
space, divided by the 99th percentile of that same distance within the training set.
Ratio > 1 means the model is extrapolating.

• Why kNN and not Mahalanobis: the features are correlated and distinctly non-Gaussian
  (origin fractions are bounded, variances are skewed). kNN distance makes no distributional
  assumption and needs no covariance inversion on 400 rows.

Result: 0 of 50 optimiser blends are inside training support. I tried the obvious fix
— force blends to look more historical by tightening the per-bale cap to 6%. It cut the
in-band rate from 0.90 to 0.82 and the saving to roughly zero, while lifting in-support
blends only to 8%. A bad trade, so I reverted it and kept the measurement. The system now
refuses to rate any such blend HIGH confidence, which makes human review mandatory —
the honest response to a model being asked to extrapolate.

[H3] RAG and the LLM — deliberately off the decision path

This is not a generative-AI use case, and pretending otherwise would be the tell.

• RAG: 18 hand-written fibre-science notes. The retriever tries a local
  sentence-transformers model and falls back to TF-IDF cosine when it is unavailable — so
  the pipeline runs on a mill VM with no GPU and no model download. The query is built
  from the run itself: yarn count, binding constraints, blend micronaire spread, SFC.
• LLM: Claude writes a 4-6 sentence rationale grounded in the retrieved notes and the
  run's numbers. It never computes or changes a number. With no API key, a
  deterministic templated rationale is used and the UI labels it as such, so the pipeline
  never blocks on a third-party service.
• Why so constrained: the rationale is for trust-building and knowledge capture. The
  moment an LLM is on the decision path, the decision stops being reproducible — and every
  governance control in this project depends on reproducibility.
• No agents. A weekly blend must be deterministic and auditable. If an agentic layer is
  ever added, it belongs above these deterministic tools — a what-if assistant that calls
  run_optimiser / compare_scenarios and never invents numbers itself.

[H3] Governance — the part that actually changed the outcome

• Model registry (plain JSON, human-readable): every model ever proposed, its metrics,
  status (candidate / approved / retired), approver, timestamp, CAB note.
• Training produces a candidate only. Earlier, training silently overwrote the
  "current" pointer, meaning an unapproved model was serving. Now the app and both
  optimisers refuse to start without an approved model.
• Promotion gate (make promote APPROVER="<name>"): acceptance thresholds first
  (not overridable — false-accept rate is the hard gate, because shipping out-of-spec
  yarn costs more than any cotton saving), then the regression suite. A regression can be
  accepted only with a recorded reason from a named human, written into the registry.
  It refused my own promotion until I wrote that reason.
• Golden set: the 50 newest laydowns, never trained on, with ground-truth physics for
  decision metrics.
• Drift monitor: PSI per key feature against a training-distribution snapshot stored
  inside the model artifact, rolling MAE vs the model's own golden MAE, logged master
  corrections, and unseen origins. (The PSI window is 100 laydowns deliberately: PSI has a
  sampling floor of roughly (bins−1)/n, so a small window would alarm on noise.)
• CI: data → train → golden set → regression gate → tests, on every push.
• Audit log: every recommendation and decision, with confidence level and the reasons
  behind it, in SQLite.

[H3] Testing strategy

40 tests, and they are not accuracy assertions:

• Property tests on the physics: the SFC penalty must accelerate; micronaire variance
  must hurt evenness. These encode domain claims, so a careless edit to the ground truth
  fails loudly.
• Invariance tests: scaling all weights by 7 must not change the blend profile.
• Constraint tests: LP output must respect the cardinality, drift and origin-cap
  constraints; both optimisers must refuse stale prices.
• Governance tests: promotion must refuse a threshold failure even with a CAB reason,
  must refuse a regression without one, and must retire the previous approved model.
• Drift tests: quiet on historical data, fires on a simulated crop-year shift.

[H2] Results, and how they shrank

Golden set: 50 held-out laydowns, model v2.

• CSP MAE / label-noise floor — Result: 22.9 / 28; Gate: ≤ 35
• P10-P90 coverage (CSP / U% / IPI / ends) — Result: 0.86 / 0.86 / 0.76 / 0.80; Gate: 0.70-0.90
• Decision agreement with ground truth — Result: 0.94; Gate: ≥ 0.90
• False-accept rate — Result: 0.02; Gate: ≤ 0.05
• Optimiser lands in spec band — Result: 0.90 (95% CI 0.79-0.96); Gate: ≥ 0.90
• Saving at strictly matched quality — Result: 3.0% (95% CI 0.8-5.5%, n=35); Gate: 1-4% planning range
• Blends inside training support — Result: 0 of 50; Gate: —

Where the old 6.4% went. It was measured on a 15-scenario sample with 11 matched
cases. Re-run on all 50 golden laydowns with the same optimiser, it is 3.2%. Adding the
contamination cap costs a further ~0.2 points. Nothing about the model got worse; the
measurement got honest. A small sample plus a favourable configuration is how most
portfolio projects — and a fair number of vendor decks — produce impressive numbers.

[H2] Honest limitations

• Synthetic data throughout. The physics surrogate is directionally right, not a mill.
• Every optimiser blend is out of training support. Mitigated by mandatory human review,
  not solved.
• The in-band rate sits exactly on its 0.90 threshold; its interval reaches down to 0.79.
• The LLM layer is the least-governed part: the prompt forbids inventing figures, but
  nothing yet verifies that the numbers in the rationale match the run. That check — and
  hashing the prompt and knowledge base into the audit log — is the next thing I will build.
• Single plant, four counts, no machine integration, no price forecasting.

[H2] Stack

• Data — Tool: pandas, pyarrow, Parquet; Why this one: Columnar, typed, reproducible; laydowns carry list-valued bale IDs
• Model — Tool: LightGBM; Why this one: Small tabular data with interactions; native quantile objective; SHAP
• Uncertainty — Tool: scikit-learn (KFold) + custom CQR; Why this one: Distribution-free coverage on 400 rows
• Support / drift — Tool: scikit-learn NearestNeighbors, PSI; Why this one: No distributional assumptions
• MILP — Tool: PuLP + CBC; Why this one: Free, deterministic, shadow prices
• Metaheuristic — Tool: DEAP; Why this one: Optimises against the full non-linear model
• Retrieval — Tool: sentence-transformers → TF-IDF fallback; Why this one: Runs with or without a GPU/model download
• LLM — Tool: Claude (Anthropic SDK); Why this one: Rationale only; templated fallback keeps the pipeline offline-safe
• App — Tool: Streamlit; Why this one: One-screen review is the whole HITL requirement
• Audit — Tool: SQLite; Why this one: Append-only in practice, zero ops
• CI — Tool: GitHub Actions; Why this one: The regression gate has to run somewhere
• Docs — Tool: Playwright; Why this one: Screenshots and carousel regenerate from code
