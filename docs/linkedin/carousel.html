The decision

Raw cotton is 55–65%
of yarn cost. One person
decides it every week.

A mixing master picks which bales go into the laydown and in what proportions. Ring-spun cotton, 20s–60s Ne — the workhorse range of Indian spinning. He is balancing four things at once:
  • Cost — bales differ in ₹/kg by origin, grade and lot.
  • Quality — CSP, U%, imperfections and ends-down must land inside the count's spec band.
  • Consistency — the fibre profile must stay near the rolling average, or count and dye affinity wander between lots.
  • Contamination risk — polypropylene and hair travel with certain origins and cannot be cleaned out. They surface after dyeing, in the customer's fabric.
Today this lives in one expert's head and a spreadsheet. It is not re-optimised when cotton prices move mid-week, and it leaves no record of why a blend was chosen — so when a lot goes bad nobody can reconstruct the reasoning, and when he retires it leaves with him.
The most expensive recurring decision in the plant2 / 10
Why ML, not a rule

Three non-linearities
a cost rule cannot see.

Short-fibre content penalises with an exponent > 1
A blend at 13% SFC is far worse than twice as bad as one at 9%. Strength and evenness both fall away faster than linearly.
Micronaire variance matters on its own
Mixing coarse and fine fibre hurts evenness and neps super-linearly, independently of the mean. Two blends with identical average micronaire spin differently — which is why the model gets weighted variances as explicit features.
Neps come from two causes that interact
Immature fibre and short fibre multiply each other's effect rather than adding.
So the model predicts CSP, U%, imperfections and ends-down from the blend's aggregate fibre profile — weighted means, weighted variances, origin fractions, and an effective bale count (inverse Herfindahl) that separates "45 bales evenly spread" from "45 bales, four of which are most of the mixing".
The response surface is continuous and interacting3 / 10
Architecture

The LLM is nowhere near
the decision.

Data layer
2,000 synthetic bale tests + 500 historical laydowns with actual yarn QC. One feature function shared by training, both optimisers and the app — train/serve skew made structurally impossible.
ML quality model
LightGBM mean + P10/P90 heads, calibrated with 5-fold conformalized quantile regression.
▼
Guards, then optimiser
Stale prices refused, contamination-prone origins hard-capped. Then MILP (PuLP/CBC) and/or a GA (DEAP).
Confidence policy
HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW. Out-of-support blends are never HIGH, so a human must review them.
▼
RAG + LLM explainer
18 fibre-science notes retrieved; Claude writes a 4–6 sentence rationale. Advisory only, never touches a number.

Approve / Adjust / Reject, then SQLite: recommendation, confidence, reasons, decision.

The model

Why LightGBM — and why
the intervals mattered more
than the predictions.

LightGBM, not a neural net
400 training laydowns, 15 tabular features, strong interactions. That is exactly where gradient-boosted trees beat both linear models and deep nets — a net would memorise. It also gives a native quantile objective and SHAP, both of which I needed.
The P10 is what gates auto-suggest
A blend whose mean CSP clears the floor but whose P10 does not is roughly a one-in-ten chance of shipping out-of-spec yarn. So a too-narrow band doesn't just look wrong — it manufactures false confidence in the exact gate meant to catch risk.
Conformalized quantile regression fixed it
Raw quantile heads on 400 rows overfit and came out too narrow: coverage 0.67 and 0.68 against a policy demanding 0.70–0.90. 5-fold CQR — out-of-fold conformity scores max(lo − y, y − hi), finite-sample corrected, widened additively — restored it to 0.76 and 0.80, with no change to point accuracy.
Chronological train/test split, because a random one would leak time structure and flatter the model.
Distribution-free coverage under exchangeability5 / 10
The optimiser

Two optimisers, because
neither one is enough.

MILP · PuLP + CBC

Fast and explainable

Why it can't use the real model: quality depends on weighted variances, which are quadratic in the blend weights — not expressible in a linear program. So it optimises an OLS surrogate fitted on the linear-in-w features only.

Why that's acceptable: the full model re-scores the LP's answer, and if it fell out of band the safety margins inflate and it re-solves. The surrogate gap is exposed, not hidden.

What it buys: 1–3 seconds, global optimum against the surrogate, deterministic (so the regression harness is reproducible), and shadow prices — "which constraint costs me most".

Genetic algorithm · DEAP

Exact, slower

Why: it scores candidates with the same non-linear model the mixing master will see. No surrogate gap, and it can express constraints an LP cannot — P10 floors, variance penalties.

How it stays tractable: the whole population is scored in one batched predict call per generation. Tournament selection, uniform crossover, sparsify/jitter mutation, elitist merge.

The cost: stochastic (seeded), only near-optimal, no shadow prices.

Both run the guards first. An optimiser is a machine for finding the cheapest corner of whatever space you hand it — so prices older than 7 days are refused outright, and contamination-prone origins are hard-capped regardless of price.
LP first for speed, GA to polish against the full model6 / 10
Where the LLM sits

This is not a
generative-AI use case.
Pretending otherwise
would be the tell.

What it does

RAG over 18 hand-written fibre-science notes. The query is built from the run itself: yarn count, binding constraints, micronaire spread, SFC.

Claude writes a 4–6 sentence rationale grounded in those notes and the run's numbers, naming which constraints bind and why the fibre science makes them bind.

Retrieval falls back from sentence-transformers to TF-IDF, so it runs on a mill VM with no GPU and no model download.

What it never does

It never computes or changes a number. Every figure in the rationale comes from the optimiser and the model.

With no API key it degrades to a deterministic template and the UI says so — the pipeline never blocks on a third-party service.

No agents in the decision path. A weekly blend has to be deterministic and reproducible; every governance control here depends on that. An agentic what-if layer belongs above these tools, not inside them.

The rationale exists for trust and knowledge capture: the reasoning that used to live in one person's head now gets written down, next to the decision, every week.
Advisory, off the decision path, degrades safely7 / 10
The audit

My docs described controls
my code did not have.

  • The model failed its own policy. I required 70–90% interval coverage; imperfections and ends-down sat at 67% and 68%.
  • An unapproved model was serving. Training quietly overwrote the "current" pointer, so a candidate went live without sign-off.
  • Written but never built: drift detection, an out-of-support check, a price-freshness guard, a contamination cap. All four were in the governance doc. None were in the code.
  • The optimiser exploited that. On a 20s Ne scenario it put 54.6% of the blend into the one origin a mixing master caps for contamination risk, because it was cheap.
  • CI did not exist — while the policy said the regression suite "fails CI".
This is the ordinary failure mode of ML governance: the document is the control, and nothing enforces it. Tests went from 9 to 40, and now run on every push.
Five gaps, all of them already in the docs8 / 10

What it takes to ship

The gate that refused me.

$ make promote APPROVER="Ankit Nandkishor Agrawal" decision.false_accept_rate 0.020 <= 0.05 ok quality.ends_down.pi_coverage 0.800 in [0.7,0.9] ok optimiser.in_band_rate 0.900 >= 0.9 ok regression vs baseline v1: saving_at_matched_quality 6.420 -> 3.020 REGRESSED REFUSED: fix them or record a CAB decision with --accept-regression "<reason>"
Thresholds are not overridable — false-accept rate is the hard gate, because shipping out-of-spec yarn costs more than any cotton saving. A regression can be accepted only with a written reason from a named human, which now sits in the model registry next to the version.
